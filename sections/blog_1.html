The Hidden Cost of Idle Time: Why OEE Is the Most Underused Metric in Indian Manufacturing

Most plant managers track output. Few track what the machine was doing when it wasn't producing. Overall Equipment Effectiveness is not just a KPI — it is a diagnostic lens that separates world-class factories from average ones.

Samriddha Basu
Operations & SCM Strategist · SIOM Nashik

The Metric Nobody Talks About Until It's Too Late

Walk into any mid-sized manufacturing plant in India and ask the plant head what their OEE is. You will get one of two answers: a confident number that hasn't been measured in six months, or a pause followed by "we track output against target." Neither is a good sign.

Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) is a composite metric that captures three things simultaneously — Availability (is the machine running when it should be?), Performance (is it running at the right speed?), and Quality (are the outputs good?). A world-class OEE score sits at around 85%. The average Indian manufacturing plant, depending on sector, runs between 50–65%. That gap is not inefficiency. It is cash left on the factory floor every single shift.

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. A machine that is available 90% of the time, running at 85% of rated speed, and producing 95% good parts has an OEE of just 72.7% — well below world-class, despite each individual component looking "acceptable."

Why Idle Time Is the Invisible Drain

Idle time — the period when a machine or workstation is available but not producing — is the most insidious category of loss in any manufacturing system. Unlike breakdowns, which are visible and dramatic, idle time accumulates quietly. A five-minute wait for a material transfer here. A twelve-minute changeover that should take four there. An operator waiting for a supervisor to approve a quality check. None of these feel significant in isolation. Together, they routinely account for 15–25% of available production time.

During my internship at CEAT Specialty Tires, working across NTBC and PTBM operations in Nashik, I observed exactly this pattern. Scheduling inefficiencies — mismatches between shift handovers, material availability, and machine readiness — were creating idle windows that were invisible to the standard output-versus-target reporting used by the team. The production MIS tracked what was made. It did not track what could have been made.

The Three Idle Time Traps in Indian Manufacturing

1. The Scheduling Mismatch

Production schedules are often built at the beginning of a shift and treated as fixed plans. When a machine goes down, when raw material is delayed, or when a quality rejection occurs mid-shift, the schedule does not adapt — and idle time accumulates while supervisors make manual decisions that should have been pre-planned as contingencies.

2. The Changeover Illusion

Changeover time is frequently under-reported. When an operator marks a changeover as "complete," they often mean the physical setup is done — not that the machine is running at rated speed and producing good parts. The ramp-up period, which can add 20–40% to effective changeover time, is rarely captured in standard MIS reporting.

3. The Minor Stoppage Blind Spot

Stoppages under five minutes are rarely logged. Yet across a ten-hour shift, a machine experiencing six such stoppages per hour has lost an entire hour of production — equivalent to 10% of available time — that appears nowhere in the downtime report.

What Good OEE Tracking Actually Requires

Effective OEE measurement is not about installing expensive SCADA systems or IoT sensors (though these help at scale). It starts with four disciplines:

  • Standardised reason codes — every stoppage, however brief, must be logged against a pre-defined reason code. This is a cultural change as much as a systems change.
  • Real-time visibility — OEE calculated at end-of-shift is historical data, not operational intelligence. Hourly OEE dashboards change operator behaviour in real time.
  • Accountability at the machine level — OEE should be visible to the operator, not just the plant manager. When people see their own data, they manage it differently.
  • Trend analysis, not point-in-time snapshots — a single day's OEE is noise. A 30-day trend by machine, shift, and product category is signal.

The Opportunity for Indian Manufacturers

India's manufacturing sector is under pressure from both sides — rising input costs and intensifying global competition. In this environment, the factories that will win are not necessarily the ones with the newest equipment. They are the ones that extract the most value from the equipment they already have.

Moving OEE from 55% to 70% on an existing production line is not a capital expenditure problem. It is a measurement, process design, and management discipline problem. The tools to do it — from Power BI dashboards to structured shift planning frameworks — are accessible. The willingness to treat idle time as a strategic priority is what separates factories that grow from factories that plateau.

Every percentage point of OEE improvement on a high-utilisation line translates directly to throughput gain without additional capital investment. In a 200-machine plant running two shifts, a 5% OEE improvement can be worth crores of rupees in annual output.

A Practitioner's Starting Point

If you are an operations manager reading this and wondering where to start — begin with one line. Instrument it properly. Track reason codes religiously for 30 days. Build an honest OEE baseline. What you find will almost certainly surprise you, and it will give you a roadmap that no consultant can build better than your own data.

The hidden cost of idle time is not hidden at all. It is sitting in your production log, waiting to be seen.
