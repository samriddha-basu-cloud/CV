The Digital Twin Imperative: Why Indian Manufacturers Can No Longer Afford to Wait

Samriddha Basu
MBA — Operations & SCM · SIOM Nashik
April 28, 2026  ·  8 min read

India's manufacturing ambitions are significant. Under Make in India 2.0, the government targets manufacturing contributing 25% of GDP and creating 100 million jobs by 2025. The challenge is not intent — it is execution infrastructure. And one of the most decisive infrastructure gaps is the absence of real-time operational intelligence at the plant level.

What Is a Digital Twin — and Why Does It Matter on the Shop Floor?

A digital twin is a virtual, real-time replica of a physical system — a machine, a production line, a warehouse, or an entire supply chain. By mirroring the physical world in software, operators and planners gain the ability to simulate scenarios, detect anomalies, and predict failures before they happen. The difference between a digital twin and a conventional dashboard is this: dashboards tell you what happened. Digital twins tell you what will happen — and let you test interventions before committing them to the shop floor.

For a production planner or operations manager, this is transformative. Instead of responding to a machine breakdown after it has already cost four hours of throughput, a digital twin equipped with predictive maintenance models can flag degradation patterns 48 to 72 hours in advance. Instead of waiting for end-of-shift reports to identify a bottleneck, a real-time virtual model surfaces it as it develops.

Key Insight

Companies deploying digital twins in manufacturing report OEE improvements of 10–20% within the first 18 months of implementation — primarily driven by reduced unplanned downtime and improved shift planning accuracy.

The Indian Context: Where Does Digital Twin Adoption Stand?

Despite the global momentum, digital twin adoption in Indian manufacturing remains concentrated in a handful of large-cap companies — Tata Motors, Mahindra, and a few pharmaceutical majors. The mid-market and MSME segments, which account for the bulk of India's manufacturing employment, are largely still operating on manual reporting cycles, spreadsheet-based planning, and experience-driven decision-making.

This is not simply a technology problem. It is a data infrastructure problem. A digital twin is only as good as the real-time data feeding it. And for most Indian manufacturers, the foundational layer — sensors on machines, networked PLCs, structured data pipelines from the shop floor to the ERP — is either absent or incomplete. Building the twin before building this foundation is, to use a supply chain metaphor, trying to run a JIT system without a reliable supplier.

Three Entry Points for Mid-Market Manufacturers

The good news is that digital twin implementation does not require a greenfield investment. There are three pragmatic entry points for manufacturers who want to begin building operational intelligence without a full-scale overhaul.

1. Machine-level digital twins. Start with the most critical, highest-downtime machine in your plant. Instrument it with IoT sensors measuring temperature, vibration, cycle time, and energy consumption. Connect the data to a simple ML model trained on failure precursors. This gives you a predictive maintenance system for one asset — and a proof of concept to build on.

2. Production line simulation. Use historical production data — even from Excel-based logs — to build a discrete event simulation of your key production line. Tools like AnyLogic or even Python-based SimPy can create a model that lets planners test different batch sizes, shift configurations, and maintenance schedules before implementing them. This is a digital twin in function even if not branded as one.

3. Supply chain visibility layer. Map your supply chain — suppliers, lead times, buffer stocks, logistics nodes — into a digital model. Use it to simulate what happens when a supplier misses a delivery or when demand spikes by 30%. This kind of scenario planning is a digital twin application that requires no hardware investment, only data discipline.

Perspective

The question is no longer whether Indian manufacturers will adopt digital twin technology — it is who will build the capability to implement it well. The operations professionals who understand both the physical system and the data architecture will define competitive advantage in the next decade.

What This Means for Operations Professionals

The shift to digital twin-enabled operations changes the profile of what an effective operations manager looks like. Technical literacy — the ability to read data pipelines, understand model outputs, and translate sensor data into operational decisions — is no longer optional. The operations professionals who thrive in this environment will be those who can hold a conversation with both the machine operator and the data engineer.

For students and early-career professionals in operations, the implication is clear: build the hybrid competency. Understand the process deeply — the physics of a production line, the logic of an inventory model, the economics of a supply chain — and pair it with the ability to work with data systems. The intersection is where the leverage lives.

Conclusion

India's manufacturing sector has the scale, the ambition, and increasingly the talent to become a global operations leader. Digital twin technology is one of the most powerful tools available to close the gap between current operational reality and that ambition. The manufacturers who begin building the data foundation today — even at the machine level, even through simple simulation — will be best positioned to scale when the technology matures further and costs continue to fall.

The imperative is not to implement digital twins perfectly from day one. It is to start.
